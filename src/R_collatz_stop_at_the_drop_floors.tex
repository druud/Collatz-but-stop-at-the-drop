\begin{center}\textbf{Abstract}\end{center}
\begin{quotation}\small\noindent
Under the map $T(x) = x/2$ for even $x$ and $(3x+1)/2$ for odd $x$, the starts that first fall below
themselves after $n$ odd steps do so after $L = p_2(n)$ steps, $p_2(n)$ the bit length of $3^n$, and
on each of their classes modulo $2^L$ the map $T^L$ is affine with slope $\theta_n = 3^n/2^L$: the
floor of Order $n$. The floors lie in the octave $[1/2, 1)$. We show that neighbouring floors of the
Orders $0$ to $N$ differ by at most three ratios $3^k/2^l$ or $2^l/3^k$, the largest the product of the
other two: the intervals of Pythagorean tuning, from the fifth and the fourth at $N = 1$ to the
limma, the apotome and the comma at $N = 12$. The floors decide the counts of the drop words: the
words of length $L$ with $n$ ones that are no rotation of a drop word are counted by the Orders whose
floors lie above $\theta_n$, there are none exactly at the upper record Orders, and their share tends
to a staircase in $\log_2\theta_n$. The three gaps and the counts are proved in Lean~4.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{MSC2020:} 11B83 (primary); 11J71, 11A55, 05A15.\\
\textbf{Keywords:} Collatz problem, $3x+1$ problem, parity vectors, three-distance theorem,
continued fractions, Pythagorean tuning, cycle lemma, Lean~4.
\end{quotation}

\section*{Main results}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Main results}

Drop words, their Order $n$ and length $L = p_2(n)$ are defined in Section~\ref{sec:words}. Write
$N(n)$ for the number of drop words of Order $n$ (OEIS A100982~\cite{OEIS_A100982}),
$\theta_n = 3^n/2^{p_2(n)}$ for the floor of Order $n$, $e_n = p_2(n) - n\log_2 3 = -\log_2\theta_n$, and
$\alpha = \log_2 3$.

\begin{mainthm}[The three gaps; Section~\ref{sec:gaps}]
For every $N \ge 1$, the floors $\theta_0, \dots, \theta_N$, put on the octave as a circle ($1$ and $1/2$
the same point), cut it into arcs whose end ratios take at most three values: $2\theta_a$ and $1/\theta_b$,
$a$ and $b$ the last lower and upper record Orders up to $N$, and their product. The product is missing
exactly when $N + 1$ is a record Order.
\end{mainthm}

\begin{mainthm}[The counts; Section~\ref{sec:counts}]
For $n \ge 1$ and $L = p_2(n)$, the words of length $L$ with $n$ ones none of whose rotations is a drop
word number
\[
\binom{L}{n} - L\,N(n) = \sum_{m} N(m)\binom{p_2(n-m)}{n-m},
\]
the sum over the $1 \le m < n$ with $\theta_m > \theta_n$ (OEIS A400061). They are none exactly at the
upper record Orders $1, 3, 5, 17, 29, 41, 94, \dots$, where $\theta_n$ is above every earlier floor.
\end{mainthm}

\begin{mainthm}[The staircase; Section~\ref{sec:stair}]
With $p = 1/\alpha$ and $G(e) = 1 - \sum_{m \ge 1,\, e_m < e} N(m)\,p^m(1-p)^{p_2(m)-m}$:
$L\,N(n)/\binom{L}{n} - G(e_n) = O(n^{-1/2})$ as $n \to \infty$.
\end{mainthm}

\subsection*{What was known}
The three-distance theorem, conjectured by Steinhaus, was proved first by
\'Swierczkowski~\cite{Swierczkowski1959}, then by Erd\H{o}s, Sz\"usz, Haj\'os and S\'os
(Sur\'anyi~\cite[\S1]{Suranyi1958}; S\'os~\cite{Sos1958} gave the order of the points by their adjacent
multiples); Sur\'anyi~\cite{Suranyi1958} gave it by the Farey sequence,
and Theorem~A is his, read on the floors. The record Orders
as the semiconvergent denominators of $\alpha$ are in paper~2~\cite{Paper2}, formalized in Lean;
Theorem~B and Theorem~C are Theorems~5.5, 5.9 and~5.10 of the drop zeta paper~\cite{ZetaPaper}, the rate
$O(n^{-1/2})$ in Theorem~C Winkler's (Theorem~5.10 there), the bound
$L\,N(n) \le \binom{L}{n}$ with its equality case Winkler's~\cite{Winkler2026}. Pythagorean tuning as
the three-gap case of the circle of fifths is classical in music theory: Carey and
Clampitt~\cite{CareyClampitt1989} call a scale of $B$ fifths well formed, and show that it is so exactly
when $B$ is a convergent or semiconvergent denominator of $\log_2 3$ (their Characterization Theorem),
which is the case of Theorem~A without the product: $B$ a record Order. New here is the reading on the floors: the
end ratios as Pythagorean intervals, counted by the last lower and upper record Orders, and the counts
of Theorem~B and the staircase of Theorem~C on the same picture.

\section{The picture}
\label{sec:picture}

% In paper 1's style: what a floor is, read off Figure 1, no proofs.
Pick a whole number and play the game of paper~1~\cite{Paper1}: \emph{even}: halve it;
\emph{odd}: triple it, add one, and halve the result. Stop as soon as you are below the start: the
drop. From $5$: $5 \to 8 \to 4$, one odd step, and $4/5$ of the start is left. Every fourth number
$1, 5, 9, 13, \dots$ plays the same two steps, and $4i + 1$ lands on $3i + 1$. What is left of the start,
\[
\tfrac{1}{1}, \quad \tfrac{4}{5}, \quad \tfrac{7}{9}, \quad \tfrac{10}{13}, \quad \dots,
\quad \tfrac{3i+1}{4i+1}, \quad \dots,
\]
gets smaller and smaller, closer and closer to $3/4$, but never gets there. The family $16i + 3$, with
two odd steps ($3 \to 5 \to 8 \to 4 \to 2$), lands on $9i + 2$: closer and closer to $9/16$. With $n$
odd steps a family keeps at least $3^n/2^L$ of its start, $L$ the number of steps: the \emph{floor} of
the families with $n$ odd steps, their \emph{Order}. The number of steps is fixed by the Order: $2^L$ is
the first power of $2$ above $3^n$. So every floor lies between $1/2$ and $1$: in one octave.

Put one dot per Order on that octave, Order $n$ at height $n$ (Figure~\ref{fig:floors}). The dots look
scattered, but they lie on lines. Twelve Orders up, a dot moves a little to the right, by the factor
$3^{12}/2^{19}$: the Pythagorean comma of music, as twelve fifths ($3/2$) overshoot seven octaves by it.
Five Orders up, a dot moves to the left, by the factor $2^8/3^5 = 256/243$: the limma, the half tone of
Pythagorean tuning. A red dot is further right than every dot below it: those Orders $1, 3, 5, 17, 29,
\dots$ have the highest floor so far. A blue dot is further left than every dot below it: $1, 2, 7, 12,
\dots$.

Now look at the gaps between neighbouring dots, side by side, of the Orders $0$ to $12$. Measured as
ratios, there are only three sizes: the limma $256/243$, the apotome $3^7/2^{11}$, and the comma, and
the apotome is the limma times the comma. Those are the steps of the Pythagorean scale. For any number
of Orders there are never more than three sizes (Theorem~A), and the red and blue Orders are where a new
size comes in.

The floors also count words. Write a family as its odd and even steps, \texttt{1} and \texttt{0}: the
family $4i + 1$ is \texttt{10}, and $16i + 3$ is \texttt{1100}. For the red Orders, and only for them,
every arrangement of $n$ ones and $L - n$ zeros, read around a circle, can be started at a point where
it reads as a family (Theorem~B).

\section{Words, Orders and floors}
\label{sec:words}

Let $T(x) = (3x+1)/2$ for odd $x$ and $T(x) = x/2$ for even $x$. A \emph{word} is a finite $0/1$ word,
the parities of successive values; the \emph{level} of a word with $a$ ones among $j$ letters is
$3^a/2^j$.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{drop word} is a nonempty word whose proper nonempty prefixes all have level above $1$ and
whose level is below $1$. Its number of ones is its \emph{Order}; its length is then $L = p_2(n)$, the
bit length of $3^n$ ($p_2(0) = 1$; OEIS A020914). The \emph{floor} of Order $n$ is
$\theta_n = 3^n/2^{p_2(n)}$.
\end{definition}

For a start $x$ whose first $L$ parities are a drop word $w$ of Order $n$,
$2^L T^L(x) = 3^n x + \rho_w$ with an integer $\rho_w \ge 0$ (Terras~\cite{Terras1976}), so
$T^L(x)/x = \theta_n + \rho_w/(2^L x)$ falls towards $\theta_n$ along the class: the floor is the
infimum of the drop ratios of the Order. $\theta_0 = 1/2$, and $1/2 < \theta_n < 1$ for $n \ge 1$;
$e_n = -\log_2\theta_n = 1 - \{n\alpha\}$ for $n \ge 1$.

\begin{definition}
An Order $n \ge 1$ is an \emph{upper record Order} if $\theta_n > \theta_m$ for all $1 \le m < n$, and a
\emph{lower record Order} if $\theta_n < \theta_m$ for all $1 \le m < n$.
\end{definition}

The upper record Orders are $1, 3, 5, 17, 29, 41, 94, 147, 200, 253, 306, 971, \dots$, the lower ones
$1, 2, 7, 12, 53, 359, 665, \dots$: the denominators of the upper and lower semiconvergents of
$\alpha$ (paper~2~\cite{Paper2}, \lean{upperRecord\_iff\_semiconv}, \lean{lowerRecord\_iff\_semiconv}).

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:split}
For $1 \le m < n$: $p_2(m) + p_2(n - m) - p_2(n) \in \{0, 1\}$, and it is $0$ iff $\theta_m > \theta_n$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
As in the zeta paper~\cite[Theorem~5.5]{ZetaPaper}. Put $a = p_2(m)$, $b = p_2(n-m)$. As
$2^{a-1} < 3^m < 2^a$ and $2^{b-1} < 3^{n-m} < 2^b$, $2^{a+b-2} < 3^n < 2^{a+b}$, so $p_2(n)$ is $a + b - 1$
or $a + b$. Next, $\theta_m > \theta_n$ iff $2^{p_2(n)} > 2^a 3^{n-m}$. For $p_2(n) = a + b$ this holds, as
$3^{n-m} < 2^b$; for $p_2(n) = a + b - 1$ it fails, as $3^{n-m} > 2^{b-1}$.
\end{proof}

\section{The three gaps}
\label{sec:gaps}

Put the octave $[1/2, 1)$ on a circle, $1$ and $1/2$ the same point. For $N \ge 1$, the floors
$\theta_0, \dots, \theta_N$ are distinct and cut the circle into $N + 1$ arcs; the \emph{end ratio} of
an arc from $\theta$ up to the next floor $\theta'$ is $\theta'/\theta$, and of the arc from the
highest floor $\theta$ round to $\theta_0 = 1/2$ it is $1/\theta$. For $N \ge 1$ let $a_N$ and $b_N$ be the
last lower and the last upper record Order up to $N$: $\theta_{a_N}$ is the lowest of
$\theta_1, \dots, \theta_N$, and $\theta_{b_N}$ the highest.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:gaps}
Let $N \ge 1$, $a = a_N$, $b = b_N$. The end ratios of the floors $\theta_0, \dots, \theta_N$ are
\[
2\theta_a = \frac{3^a}{2^{p_2(a)-1}} \ (N - a + 1 \text{ times}), \qquad
\frac{1}{\theta_b} = \frac{2^{p_2(b)}}{3^b} \ (N - b + 1 \text{ times}), \qquad
\frac{2\theta_a}{\theta_b} \ (a + b - N - 1 \text{ times}).
\]
So there are at most three, the largest the product of the other two, and exactly two iff $N + 1 = a + b$,
iff $N + 1$ is a record Order: $a + b$ is the next record Order after $N$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On the logarithmic octave, $\theta$ at $\log_2\theta + 1$ of a turn, the floor of Order $n$ sits at
$\{n\alpha\} = \{n\alpha'\}$, $\alpha' = \alpha - 1 \in (0, 1)$, irrational (Order $0$ at $0$), and the end ratio
of an arc is $2$ to the power of its length. Let $l/k < \alpha' < l'/k'$ be the neighbours of $\alpha'$ in the
Farey sequence of order $N$. By Sur\'anyi~\cite[Satz~I]{Suranyi1958}, $\{k\alpha'\}$ is the least and
$\{k'\alpha'\}$ the largest of $\{\alpha'\}, \dots, \{N\alpha'\}$: $k = a$ and $k' = b$. By his Satz~II, the point
after $P_m = \{m\alpha'\}$ ($0 \le m \le N$) on the circle is $P_{m+a}$ for $m \le N - a$, $P_{m-b}$ for $m \ge b$,
and $P_{m+a-b}$ for $N - a < m < b$; the arcs have the lengths of the arcs from $P_0$ to $P_a$, from $P_b$
to $P_0$, and from $P_b$ through $P_0$ to $P_a$~\cite[\S7]{Suranyi1958}, with the end ratios
$\theta_a/\theta_0 = 2\theta_a$, $1/\theta_b$, and their product. The three ranges of $m$ have
$N - a + 1$, $N - b + 1$ and $a + b - N - 1 \ge 0$ members. The Farey neighbours of $\alpha'$ stay the same
until the order reaches $a + b$, where the mediant $(l + l')/(a + b)$ comes in between them; so the
least or the largest of the $\{n\alpha'\}$ changes first at $n = a + b$: the next record Order after $N$ is
$a + b$.
\end{proof}

So the gaps change only at the record Orders: at a lower record Order $N$ the ratio $2\theta_N$ comes
in, at an upper one $1/\theta_N$, and the product of the two current ones disappears just before the next
record Order. \'Swierczkowski's Corollary~1~\cite{Swierczkowski1959} has the same three lengths, the third
only for $N < a + b - 1$ (his $a_r$ and $a_k$ are our $a$ and $b$), and S\'os~\cite[\S4]{Sos1958} the same counts in terms of the adjacent multiples of
$\alpha'$, the convergent and semiconvergent denominators~\cite[\S5]{Sos1958}: the record Orders of
paper~2~\cite{Paper2}. Table~\ref{tab:gaps} shows the first changes.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:unimod}
The last records $a = a_N$, $b = b_N$ satisfy $a\,p_2(b) - b\,(p_2(a) - 1) = 1$. For $N = 1$, $a = b = 1$.
At the next record Order $c = a + b$: if it is a lower one, $\theta_c = \theta_a\theta_b$,
$p_2(c) = p_2(a) + p_2(b)$, and the new pair $(c, b)$ gives $c\,p_2(b) - b\,(p_2(c) - 1) = a\,p_2(b) -
b\,(p_2(a) - 1)$; if an upper one, $\theta_c = 2\theta_a\theta_b$, $p_2(c) = p_2(a) + p_2(b) - 1$, and
$(a, c)$ gives $a\,p_2(c) - c\,(p_2(a) - 1) = a\,p_2(b) - b\,(p_2(a) - 1)$ again. So successive upper
records $u < v$ have $v\,p_2(u) - u\,p_2(v) = 1$: the vectors $(u, p_2(u))$ and $(v, p_2(v))$ span the
integer lattice.
\end{remark}

\section{Floors and counts}
\label{sec:counts}

Fix $n \ge 1$, $L = p_2(n)$, and let $W(n)$ be the $\binom{L}{n}$ words of length $L$ with $n$ ones. The
\emph{rotation} of $u = u_0 \cdots u_{L-1}$ by $r$ is $u_r \cdots u_{L-1} u_0 \cdots u_{r-1}$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:counts}
\[
\binom{L}{n} = L\,N(n) + \sum_{\substack{1 \le m < n\\ \theta_m > \theta_n}} N(m)\binom{p_2(n-m)}{n-m}.
\]
The words of $W(n)$ none of whose rotations is a drop word number the sum (OEIS A400061), and they are
none iff $n$ is an upper record Order.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The zeta paper~\cite[Theorem~5.5]{ZetaPaper} proves the identity with the sum over the
$1 \le m < n$ with $p_2(m) + p_2(n-m) = p_2(n)$, that is with $\theta_m > \theta_n$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:split}. A drop word of Order $n$ has $L$ distinct rotations, and no two drop words are
rotations of each other, as no rotation of a drop word by $0 < s < L$ is a drop
word~\cite[Lemma~5.4\,(i)]{ZetaPaper}; so $L\,N(n)$ words of $W(n)$ are rotations of drop words, and the rest number the sum. The sum
is empty iff $\theta_n > \theta_m$ for all $1 \le m < n$, and a nonempty sum is positive, as
$N(m) \ge 1$.
\end{proof}

So the words with no drop rotation are counted by the floors above $\theta_n$: one term for each Order
$m < n$ whose dot in Figure~\ref{fig:floors} lies to the right of the dot of $n$. The equality case,
$L\,N(n) = \binom{L}{n}$ exactly at the upper record Orders, is Winkler's~\cite{Winkler2026}, by the cycle
lemma. The terms do not count the words with no drop rotation one by one: for $n = 2$ these are
$1010$ and $0101$, while the term of $m = 1$ counts $10$ followed by a word of $W(1)$, $1010$ and
$1001$~\cite[Remark~5.7]{ZetaPaper}.

\section{The staircase}
\label{sec:stair}

Read the words of $W(n)$ with equal weights. Every word passes below level $1$ before its end, and
the first time it does ends a drop word, of some Order $m \le n$; the chance that this is Order $m$ is
\[
P_n(m) = N(m)\binom{L - p_2(m)}{n - m}\Big/\binom{L}{n},
\]
and these add up to $1$~\cite[Theorem~5.9]{ZetaPaper}. By Theorem~\ref{thm:counts}, the share of the words
with no drop rotation is
\[
1 - \frac{L\,N(n)}{\binom{L}{n}} = \sum_{\substack{1 \le m < n\\ \theta_m > \theta_n}} P_n(m):
\]
the chance that the first drop of a word of $W(n)$ has its floor above $\theta_n$. For a word with
independent letters, a one with probability $p = 1/\alpha$, the first drop is at Order $m$ with chance
$N(m)p^m(1-p)^{p_2(m)-m}$, and these add up to $1$~\cite[Theorem~5.8]{ZetaPaper}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:stair}
With $G(e) = 1 - \sum_{m \ge 1,\, e_m < e} N(m)\,p^m(1-p)^{p_2(m)-m}$,
$L\,N(n)/\binom{L}{n} - G(e_n) = O(n^{-1/2})$ as $n \to \infty$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The limit is the zeta paper's Theorem~5.9~\cite{ZetaPaper}: $P_n(m) \to N(m)p^m(1-p)^{p_2(m)-m}$ for each
$m$, the limits add up to $1$, so the two distributions converge in total variation (Scheff\'e's
argument), and $1 - G(e_n)$ is the same sum as above with the limits in place of $P_n(m)$, over all
$m \ge 1$ with $\theta_m > \theta_n$ ($e_m < e_n$). The rate is Winkler's~\cite[Theorem~5.10]{ZetaPaper}:
for $\theta_m > \theta_n$, $P_n(m) = w(m)\,Q(n-m)/Q(n)$ with $w(m) = N(m)p^m(1-p)^{p_2(m)-m}$ and
$Q(j) = \binom{p_2(j)}{j}p^j(1-p)^{p_2(j)-j}$, of size $j^{-1/2}$; with $w(m) = O(m^{-3/2})$ from
$L\,N(n) \le \binom{L}{n}$, the terms with $m \le n/2$, those with $n/2 < m < n$, and the tail of $G$
each add $O(n^{-1/2})$.
\end{proof}

So the share of the words with no drop rotation is, in the limit, a staircase in the position of the
floor: $1 - G$ jumps at every floor $\theta_m$, by the chance of a first drop at Order $m$, from $0$ at
the top of the octave down to $p$ at its bottom. The difference stays below $1/70$ for the Orders $100$ to
$399$~\cite[Observation~5.11 and Figure~1]{ZetaPaper}. As the floors fill the octave, the share has
$\liminf 0$ (at the upper record Orders it is $0$) and $\limsup p$.

\section{The Lean formalization}
\label{sec:lean}

The library of this series~\cite{GitHub} has, in Lean~4 with Mathlib and without \texttt{sorry}: the
record Orders as the semiconvergent denominators of $\alpha$ (paper~2~\cite{Paper2},
\lean{upperRecord\_iff\_semiconv}, \lean{lowerRecord\_iff\_semiconv}), and the identity of
Theorem~\ref{thm:counts} with its condition (\lean{choose\_p2\_eq\_split}, \lean{mem\_splitOrders\_iff}
in the module \lean{TCycleCount}). The module \lean{TFloorGaps} proves Theorem~\ref{thm:gaps},
in exact rationals and with no Farey sequence: the floors $\theta_n$ =
\lean{blockFactor}~$n$ obey the product rule $\theta_{x+y} \in \{\theta_x\theta_y, 2\theta_x\theta_y\}$
(\lean{blockFactor\_add}); the end ratio from $\theta_m$ to $\theta_{m+k}$ is $2\theta_k$, and to
$\theta_{m-k}$ it is $1/\theta_k$ (\lean{fwd\_add}, \lean{fwd\_add'}). With $\theta_a$ the lowest and $\theta_b$
the highest of $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_N$, the next floor above $\theta_m$ is $\theta_{m+a}$, $\theta_{m-b}$ or
$\theta_{m+a-b}$ in the three ranges of $m$, at the three end ratios (\lean{gap\_up}, \lean{gap\_down},
\lean{gap\_mid}), and $a + b \ge N + 1$ (\lean{add\_ge\_of\_extremes}); each case uses only the product
rule and the two extremes. The two extremes stay the same up to Order $a + b - 1$
(\lean{extremes\_until}; distinct Orders have distinct floors, \lean{blockFactor\_inj}), and $a + b$ is
the next record Order (\lean{next\_record}): $\theta_{a+b}$ is $\theta_a\theta_b < \theta_a$ or
$2\theta_a\theta_b > \theta_b$. Along the record walk the last records keep
$a\,p_2(b) - b\,(p_2(a) - 1) = 1$ (Remark~\ref{rem:unimod}; \lean{FloorWalk} in the module
\lean{TRowLength}), and with it \lean{rowLength\_eq\_min}: the largest number of parts
$m_1 + \dots + m_k = n$ with $p_2(m_1) + \dots + p_2(m_k) = p_2(n)$, the splits behind the sum of
Theorem~\ref{thm:counts}, is the least of $d\,p_2(n) - n\,(p_2(d) - 1)$ over $d \ge 1$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:stair} is not formalized; the computations are scripts.
